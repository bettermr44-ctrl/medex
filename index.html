THE PAPER CLINIC

A Family, Three Countries, and the Machinery of Manufactured Legitimacy

An investigative reconstruction of the MedEx files — the allegations, the public records, and the cases now before Nepal’s anti-corruption court.

Compiled from the MedEx case archive.

First edition — November 2026

A NOTE ON THE RECORD

This is a book of allegations, and it says so on every page that needs to.

Several people named in these chapters are the subjects of formal complaints, regulatory filings, or — in one case — a criminal prosecution before Nepal’s Special Court. Being charged is not being convicted. Being complained about is not being guilty. Nothing in this book is a judicial finding. Where a claim rests on a government registry, the registry is named. Where a claim rests on a complaint, a review, or an inference, it is labelled as such. Where a claim could not be sourced at all, it has been left out.

That last sentence is the most important editorial decision in the book, and Chapter 20 explains why.

Any person named here may respond. Corrections supported by documents will be published in the next edition.

NOTE ON TONE

This edition is angrier than the first draft. That is deliberate.

The anger is aimed at conduct that is on the record: a visa agency selling genetic tests, a “clinic network” with one VAT-registered address, a helpline that does not answer, a family that has not explained a tenfold gap between its salary and its assets. It is not aimed at unproven guilt. The difference between those two things is the whole book.

Being harsh is not the same as being reckless. A soft sentence about a hard fact is a failure of nerve; a hard sentence about a soft fact is defamation. This edition tries to be hard only where the documents are hard.

STANDARD OF PROOF

The archive this book draws from is large, and not all of it is equally strong. Rather than flatten everything into one voice of certainty, the book uses three tiers.

Tier Label used in the text What it means
1 Documented Appears in a public register, a filed court document, a published review with a traceable identifier, or a company’s own published statement. A reader can check it.
2 Alleged Asserted in a complaint, a review, or an inference drawn from documents. Not tested by a court or regulator. May be true; may not be.
3 Not established No adequate public source was found. Excluded from argument. In a few cases, named here so that readers know it was considered and rejected.

A book about financial crime is only as useful as its willingness to lose an argument. Tier 3 exists to make that possible.

CLAIMS DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED

The case archive contains material that does not survive contact with a source. This book does not repeat it. Specifically:

  • Terrorism financing. No news report, court filing, sanctions list, or regulator finding connects MedEx, Roshan Adhikari, or Ramrati Devi Mandal to terrorism financing. Claims that “all seven FATF terrorism-financing indicators are present” are assertions, not indicators, and are not used here.
  • Child trafficking. No source found.
  • The CIAA press release as proof. The archive cites CIAA Press Release #3147. At the time of the underlying research, the public URL returned 404. The prosecution itself may well exist — but a broken link is not a citation. The case is treated here as charged and reported, pending verification against the Special Court cause list.
  • Precise loss figures presented as fact. Figures such as “USD $1.8M–$4M” or “NPR 17–75 crore” are estimates built from assumed construction and investment costs. They are useful as investigative hypotheses, not as findings. Where they appear, they are marked as estimates.
  • Guilt by association. Two senior Nepali Congress politicians are named in the complainant’s filings as political connections. This book records that they are named in those filings and asserts nothing about their conduct. Being mentioned in someone else’s complaint is not evidence of anything.

Removing these claims does not weaken the case. It concentrates it on the material a regulator can actually verify: company registrations, a filed prosecution, a licensing gap, and a documented pattern of prepaid payments for services that were never delivered.

HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

The book has seven parts.

Part I — The Family sets out who the people are, what the archive says about their roles, and where the archive contradicts itself.

Part II — The Machine describes the corporate structure: three countries, four domains, nine brands, and a registered business purpose that is not medicine. It ends with an interlude on the vocabulary that holds the structure together.

Part III — Black to White is the part the title promises. It explains, at the level of typology rather than instruction, what “converting black money” means, which mechanisms the documents support, which remain hypotheses, and how an investigator would test each one. It deliberately does not function as a manual.

Part IV — The CIAA Cases reconstructs the corruption proceedings: the one-rupee tender, the newspaper that did not exist, and what suspension under Nepal’s Prevention of Corruption Act actually means.

Part V — The Scam follows a single receipt — THB 1,450 for a test that was never booked — into the business model that makes refunds structurally improbable.

Part VI — How the Case Is Built is the investigative method: bank records, suspicious-transaction reports, mutual legal assistance, asset declarations, witnesses, and falsifiability.

Part VII — Accountability maps the regulators, explains Nepal’s FATF problem without exaggeration, and closes with what the book itself is for.

Each chapter ends with a Record / Allegation / Open question box. Readers in a hurry can read only those.

PART I — THE FAMILY

1. The House at the Edge of the Map

Dhanauji is not on the way to anywhere. It sits in Dhanusha District, in Nepal’s Madhesh Province, a few hours’ drive south-east of Janakpur, close enough to the Indian border that the morning buses carry as many Bihari accents as Maithili ones. The land is flat and generous and prone to flooding. The economy is remittances, small trade, and politics — sometimes all three in the same household.

It is the kind of place where a deputy mayor is not a distant figure. She is the woman who signs the road contract, chairs the ward meeting, and knows which family’s son has gone to Malaysia and which has gone to jail. Local office here is intimate. It is also, in Nepal’s Terai, frequently lucrative — not because the salary is high, but because the municipality controls land, procurement, grants, and permissions.

Ramrati Devi Mandal became Deputy Chairperson of Dhanauji Rural Municipality in 2074 BS, and was re-elected in 2079 BS. The archive records 2,326 votes in the first election and 2,430 in the second. She is a Nepali Congress politician, twice elected, with an Election Commission candidate ID of 157974. On any ordinary reading, this is the profile of a successful local politician: durable, networked, electorally secure.

What makes her worth a book is the arithmetic that sits beside that profile.

A deputy chairperson of a rural municipality in Nepal earns, very roughly, between NPR 50,000 and NPR 100,000 a month. Call it NPR 6 to 12 lakh a year. Over two full terms — a decade — the salary alone would total somewhere between NPR 60 lakh and NPR 1.2 crore, before household expenses, before the cost of raising children, before anything is saved.

Her eldest son, meanwhile, is the founder and chief executive of a company that operates clinics, laboratories, a pharmacy, a telemedicine platform and a medical-travel business across Thailand, Singapore and Nepal. The Thai entity alone was registered with capital of THB 2,000,000. The group’s own marketing describes a “pan-Asia health care aggregator” with more than fifty sample-collection sites and thousands of diagnostic biomarkers. The archive estimates the capital required to build such a network at somewhere between NPR 17 crore and NPR 75 crore.

That estimate is an estimate. It is built from assumptions about the cost of clinic fit-out, laboratory equipment and hospital partnerships. It could be wrong by a wide margin in either direction. But the direction is not in serious dispute: the gap between a decade of municipal salary and a three-country healthcare group is not a rounding error. It is the central question of the case.

And nobody has answered it. Not the family, not the company, not the municipality, not the CIAA in public. The archive contains a Facebook photograph of a Bangkok trip and a registry entry for a company; it does not contain a single document that says where the capital came from. That silence is not proof. It is an invitation.

The complainant’s answer is that the gap is the crime. The family’s answer — if one is ever given in a courtroom — may be that the business was built with investor money, partner capital, loans, or earnings that have nothing to do with public office. Both are possible. Only documents can decide.

This book is about the documents. Not because documents are neutral — they are not, and the state that issues them is not — but because they are the last thing in this story that cannot be talked over.

Why a family, and not a company

Most financial-crime reporting starts with a corporation and works outward. This story works the other way. The corporation is the instrument; the family is the structure.

That distinction matters for three reasons.

First, trust. A family firm can move money between members on the strength of a phone call. There is no board resolution, no independent director to persuade, no minority shareholder to disclose to. In a business where the key asset is the appearance of legitimacy, the family is also the cheapest source of it: a licensed doctor brother, a public-hospital post, a mother in elected office, a father who posts proudly on Facebook.

Second, silence. Corporate records are designed to be read. Family relationships are not. When the ownership of a Thai company is 99.995 per cent Nepali, and the directors are two brothers and a nominee holding THB 100, the register tells you what it is required to tell you and nothing more. The rest lives in the family.

Third, continuity. A company can be struck off, renamed, or replaced. A family persists across jurisdictions, across decades, and across the reputational damage that would end an unrelated executive’s career. The MedEx group has already renamed one entity — MedEx MedTravel became MedEx Ventures — and runs nine brands across four domains. The family name did not change.

So the book begins with the family, not the company. Not because families are criminal — most are not — but because in this case the family is the only structure that connects every piece of paper.

The two questions

There are really only two questions in this case, and everything else is detail.

Question one: where did the capital come from?

A clinic network in three countries requires money before it requires patients. Seed capital, deposits, equipment, salaries, regulatory fees, marketing. The archive’s estimate of NPR 17–75 crore may be too high or too low, but the sum is not trivial. If the capital can be traced to legitimate investors, loans, or declared earnings, the central allegation collapses. If it cannot, the state has a case to answer.

Question two: what happened to the patients’ money?

MedEx’s model, as documented in the review record, is prepayment. A customer pays by card, in Thai baht, before a service is delivered. When the service fails, the customer is left with a receipt, a booking reference, and a company incorporated in another country. That is not necessarily fraud; many legitimate platforms prepay. But it is a model in which the incentive to deliver weakens as the cost of not delivering falls. Chapter 6 and Chapter 16 examine that incentive in detail.

Question one is about the past — the origin of wealth. Question two is about the present — the conversion of a service business into a cash-collection business. The book treats them separately, because they are proved by different documents and answerable before different regulators.

What this chapter does not claim

It does not claim that Ramrati Devi Mandal took a bribe. It does not claim that her sons’ business is a laundering vehicle. It does not claim that the family’s wealth is illegal.

It claims something narrower and more useful: that the public record currently contains a disparity large enough to require explanation, and that no explanation has been entered into that record.

That is the standard the CIAA itself applies when it opens a disproportionate-assets inquiry. It is the standard this book applies to itself.

2. Three Sons and a Firm

In the archive’s family tree, there are three sons: Roshan, Ram Ashish, and Dr. Suman Adhikari. The tree is worth stating plainly, because the rest of the case hangs from it.

Roshan Adhikari is the eldest and the operator. His public professional record describes a bachelor’s degree from Webster University in Thailand, a stint as an international marketing executive at Vejthani Hospital in Bangkok, digital marketing work at Everex Global — a Bangkok blockchain remittance startup — and a consultancy role at a firm called INDILYFE. In 2020, at the height of the pandemic, he founded MedEx. He is listed as founder, CEO or director of the Thai, Singapore and Nepali entities, and as CEO of a fourth venture, Exciting Himalayan.

The timing is worth pausing on. 2020 was the worst year in the history of medical tourism. Borders closed; elective procedures were cancelled; hospitals in Bangkok emptied their international wings. A rational entrepreneur entering that market in that year would need either a very unusual thesis or a very unusual source of capital. Roshan Adhikari’s thesis, as marketed, was the opposite of the market: a “decentralised care continuum” and a “pan-Asia health care aggregator” that would book treatment across borders while borders were shut. It is possible that this was contrarian genius. It is also possible that the clinic business was never the point. The documents do not yet say.

Ram Ashish is described in the complainant’s case summary as the second son, based in Hetauda and associated with MedEx. The public dossier in the same archive describes him instead as the father, a public-school teacher photographed in Bangkok with the family, who publicly amplifies the business: “our son Roshan Adhikari, CEO of MedEx Thailand and Nepal.” Both versions cannot be right. This book follows the case summary and flags the conflict. Before any of this is published as fact, the relationship needs to be settled from citizenship records, not from Facebook.

Dr. Suman Adhikari is the medical credential. He studied at Kathmandu Medical College, passed the NMCLE in 2025, worked as a medical officer at Kakani Primary Health Centre, and was selected for MS General Surgery at NAMS/Bir Hospital. The archive gives two different Nepal Medical Council registration numbers for him — 15536 and 24626 — which is exactly the kind of discrepancy that is harmless in a blog post and fatal in a complaint. Whichever number is correct, the strategic function of his profile is clear: a real doctor, with a real licence, in a family that sells medical services.

There is also Raju Adhikari, named in the archive both as the husband and as a brother and co-founder with NMC registration 34725, said to be a government medical officer at Mahendranagar Primary Hospital while working for MedEx Nepal. The dual-employment allegation, if true, is a clean regulatory question — public servants in Nepal are generally barred from private practice without permission — and it is the sort of question a medical council can answer with a file rather than a prosecution.

The firm as a family instrument

Put the four profiles side by side and a structure appears.

Function Person Asset contributed
Political access and local standing Ramrati Devi Mandal Elected office, party network, municipal authority
Commercial operation and international expansion Roshan Adhikari Company formation, fundraising, brand, technology
Clinical legitimacy Dr. Suman Adhikari (and, per the archive, Dr. Raju Adhikari) Medical licence, hospital employment, professional credibility
Social proof and local amplification Ram Ashish (per the case summary) Family reputation, community network, public endorsement

This is not a conspiracy diagram. It is what sociologists of the family firm call familial capital: the conversion of one kind of trust into another. A mother’s elected office converts into local standing. A son’s medical licence converts into clinical credibility. A father’s community respect converts into the assumption that the sons must be honest. None of these conversions is illegal. All of them are useful. And in a business that sells healthcare to strangers across borders, the ability to convert reputational capital into customer confidence is the whole commercial engine.

The question a regulator asks is different: not “is this a family firm?” but “did any of these conversions cross a legal line?” Did the elected office influence a contract? Did the medical licence endorse a service the licence-holder did not supervise? Did the public hospital post coexist with private work in breach of service rules?

Those are answerable questions. That is what makes them valuable.

What the structure is missing

A legitimate healthcare group of this size would normally have:

  • an independent board, or at least non-family directors;
  • a named medical director with clinical governance responsibility;
  • a compliance function for anti-money-laundering and data protection;
  • audited group accounts;
  • a published ownership structure;
  • licensed facility registrations for each site.

The public record in this archive shows none of these. The Thai company’s registered purpose is visa and passport work, not medicine. The Singapore company is registered as an online marketplace. The Nepali company is an operating entity with a registration number and little else on the public file. The clinics are serviced offices. The doctors are employed elsewhere.

An absence is not a crime. But a group that markets itself as a clinical network while its registrations describe a visa agency and a marketplace has created a gap between what it says and what it is. Chapter 4 is about that gap.

ALLEGATION - That clinical credentials were used to lend legitimacy to a business that did not itself employ the clinicians. - That a public medical officer held private commercial roles without the required permission.

3. The Matriarch’s Ledger

Nepal has an asset-declaration regime. Public officials are required to declare their property, and the CIAA is empowered to compare declarations against reality. The regime is imperfect — enforcement is uneven and politically mediated — but it exists, and it is the natural first stop in any disproportionate-assets case.

The archive does not contain Ramrati Devi Mandal’s declaration. That is the single most important missing document in the entire file. Everything else — the Facebook photographs, the Bangkok trips, the review complaints — is circumstantial until the declaration is on the table beside the registry record.

So this chapter does the only honest thing available: it sets out the arithmetic, marks the estimates as estimates, and lists what the declaration would need to show.

The income side

The complainant’s estimate is NPR 50,000–100,000 per month, or NPR 6–12 lakh per year. Over two five-year terms, NPR 60 lakh to NPR 1.2 crore.

That figure excludes:

  • household consumption over a decade;
  • the cost of raising three children;
  • any spousal income;
  • any inherited property;
  • any legitimate business income earned by family members before or during the period;
  • any loans or gifts.

A serious forensic analysis would net all of these out. A serious defence would point out that the salary figure is only one input into a household balance sheet, and that a family can legitimately accumulate wealth from many sources over twenty years. Both points are fair, and both are why the declaration matters more than the estimate.

The asset side

The archive’s estimate of NPR 17–75 crore is built from:

Item Estimated cost Basis
Thailand clinic/hospital operation USD 1–5 million Assumed fit-out and equipment costs
Singapore regional office USD 200,000–500,000 Assumed staffing and establishment
Nepal clinics (Naxal, Thamel) NPR 50 lakh–2 crore Assumed fit-out
Two sons’ private medical education NPR 1–2 crore Published MBBS fee ranges in Nepal
Properties Unknown Not documented

Every one of these numbers is an assumption. The Thai figure could be far too high if the “clinics” are, as the dossier alleges, serviced-office rooms rather than hospitals. The education figure could be too low if foreign study was involved. The property line is blank because no one has produced a land-registry extract.

This is what an investigative estimate looks like before subpoenas: a set of hypotheses dressed as arithmetic. It is useful for deciding where to look. It is not evidence.

What the declaration would show

A complete asset declaration from a two-term deputy chairperson would list:

  1. Immovable property — land, houses, plots, in whose name, acquired when, at what consideration.
  2. Movable property — vehicles, gold, livestock, machinery.
  3. Financial assets — bank balances, shares, loans advanced, insurance.
  4. Liabilities — loans taken, mortgages.
  5. Income sources — salary, rent, business, agriculture, remittances.
  6. Family members’ assets — spouse and dependent children.

Compare that to the registry record and three questions arise immediately:

  • Timing. Were assets acquired before or after the family’s business expansion? Assets that appear in 2021–2023, at the same time as MedEx’s incorporation and expansion, are the ones that matter.
  • Names. Are assets held by the office-holder, or by sons, or by a company? Beneficial ownership is the crux. A clinic registered to a son is still a family asset if the family funded it.
  • Consideration. Land purchases often record a consideration far below market value. A Rs 40 lakh plot registered at Rs 4 lakh is a classic discrepancy and a legitimate CIAA question.

None of these questions requires a belief that anyone is guilty. They require a declaration and a land-registry extract. That is the point.

The three-case arithmetic

The archive records that three CIAA cases at the municipality total NPR 6,179,402 in claims, and that one case — Special Court Case #1 — involves a claim of NPR 3,134,778 against Ramrati Devi Mandal personally.

Those are not the same kind of number as the NPR 17–75 crore wealth estimate. They are much smaller. They relate to specific transactions at the municipality, not to the family’s entire balance sheet. Conflating the two would be a serious error: it would make a Rs 31 lakh procurement case sound like a Rs 75 crore laundering case, and a court would notice.

The correct reading is that the CIAA cases are about public money at the municipality, while the disproportionate-assets question is about the family’s private balance sheet. They are related only if the public money can be traced into the private assets. That tracing is the whole ballgame.

The honest conclusion

On the present record, the most that can be said is this:

A two-term local official’s family operates a multi-jurisdiction healthcare business whose capital requirements appear, on conservative assumptions, to exceed the family’s identifiable declared income by at least one order of magnitude. The official’s asset declaration has not been produced. Until it is, the disparity is a question, not a finding.

That sentence would survive a lawyer’s review. Most of the material in the wider archive would not. Chapter 20 explains how to move from the sentence to the finding.

PART II — THE MACHINE

4. A Clinic That Is Not a Clinic

Here is the sentence that reorganised the case, taken from the archive’s own research memo:

The public registration record classifies MedEx Ventures as a personal-services company doing visa, passport and migrant-worker nationality-verification work. It does not show a healthcare, clinic or laboratory classification.

That is not a reviewer’s claim or an anonymous tip. It is what the Thai company register says about the company that operates medex.co.th.

The entry is specific. MedEx Ventures Co., Ltd., juristic ID 0105563116815, registered 10 August 2020, registered capital THB 2,000,000, classified under TSIC 96309 — other personal service activities, not elsewhere classified. The registered business description, in Thai, translates as processing visas, renewing passports, and verifying the nationality of foreign migrant workers.

No clinic. No laboratory. No hospital. A visa agency.

Read that again. The company selling HIV panels, genetic tests and nurse-at-home visits is registered to process visas, renew passports, and check whether migrant workers are really Nepali. Not “also” registered for those things. Registered for them instead of medicine.

The caveat that must come first

A Thai company can register several business objects, and public mirrors of the register often display only the primary classification. So the correct statement is not “MedEx is legally forbidden to practise medicine.” It is:

The public registration record describes a visa and migrant-worker documentation business. It does not describe a healthcare provider.

The stronger claim — that MedEx has no licence to operate a clinic — can only be made after pulling the full company profile and checking the sanatorium and laboratory licence registers. That check is a yes/no question to the Department of Health Service Support and the Medical Technology Council. As of the archive’s research, it had not been answered.

This distinction is the difference between a finding and a suspicion. The book will keep it.

What the company says it is

5. The Architecture of Distance

There is a principle in cross-border commerce that has nothing to do with crime: distance is a cost, and costs are borne by whoever is furthest away. A company that sells across borders can make its obligations distant while keeping its revenue close. A customer who wants a refund must cross the same distance in the opposite direction, at their own expense.

Legitimate multinationals manage this with escrow, bonding, insurance, chargeback rules and local entities. The MedEx structure does something else. It arranges the distance so that the customer always has further to travel than the company does.

Three jurisdictions, one family

Layer Entity What it is for
Thailand MedEx Ventures Co., Ltd. (DBD 0105563116815) Legal contracting party on the domains; holds the Thai bank relationships; employs staff; registered as a visa/personal-services business
Singapore MEDEX VENTURES PTE. LTD. (UEN 202223804C) Regional/marketplace entity; serviced-office address; SSIC 63202
Nepal MedEx Ventures Pvt. Ltd. (OCR 210313) Local operating company; Nepali patients and staff; reachable by Nepali regulators

The three entities share a founder, a brand, a set of domains and a family. They do not obviously share capital, governance, or audited consolidated accounts — at least, none is in the public record.

This is the standard layering architecture of a cross-border service group. In a lawful business it produces tax efficiency, regulatory arbitrage and market access. In an unlawful one it produces the same things, plus deniability. The structure itself proves nothing. What matters is the direction of the money inside it.

Brand fragmentation

Four domains: medex.co, medex.co.th, medex.com.np, jivi.co. Nine brands. One Thai juristic person behind most of it.

The commercial logic of multiple brands is real: different markets, different price points, different search terms, different regulatory postures. A Nepali patient searching for a Kathmandu clinic and a Bangkok patient searching for an STD test may respond to different names.

The reputational logic is also real. A complaint about “MedEx” lands on one domain. A complaint about “JIVI” lands on another. A review of “MedEx Neo Clinic” sits under a Google listing that may not be linked to the corporate entity at all. Fragmentation does not hide the company from a determined regulator — the domains resolve to the same juristic person — but it does hide it from the ordinary consumer, who is the person the law of consumer protection is meant to protect.

Why distance is the product

Strip away the branding and the structure does one thing: it puts the customer on the wrong side of every boundary.

  • A Thai customer complaining about a Singapore contracting party must use Singapore law.
  • A Nepali customer complaining about a Thai company must use Thai law.
  • A card issuer evaluating a chargeback sees a merchant in one country, a service in another, and a beneficiary in a third.
  • A police officer in Kathmandu sees a company registered in Bangkok.
  • A regulator in Bangkok sees a company whose business description is visa processing.
  • A regulator in Singapore sees a marketplace with no capital.

Every one of those boundaries is lawful. Every one of them adds friction. Enough friction, applied to a THB 1,450 transaction, produces the same result as a legal prohibition: the customer gives up.

Distance is not a side effect of this structure. Distance is the structure’s only job. Everything else — the brands, the domains, the serviced offices — is decoration on a machine for making pursuit expensive.

6. What Was Actually Being Sold

The website sells health. Read the product list closely, because the product list is where the business model becomes visible.

  • STD panels — prepaid, booked online.
  • HIV testing, PEP and PrEP — time-sensitive, often urgent.
  • NIPT genetic testing — expensive, results delivered weeks later.
  • Nurse-at-home services — dispatched through an app.
  • Teleconsults — a consultation, then a referral.
  • Vaccinations and health check-ups — commodity medical services.
  • Medical travel — booking treatment at partner hospitals abroad.

Every one of these shares a feature: the customer pays before the service, and the service is delivered by someone else. The clinic does not manufacture the test. The laboratory does. The hospital does the surgery. The nurse is a contractor. MedEx is the intermediary — the marketplace, the booking layer, the brand.

Intermediaries are legitimate and often useful. But an intermediary business has a specific vulnerability: its revenue arrives before its costs, and its costs are owed to third parties it does not control. If the intermediary pays the laboratory, the service happens. If it does not, the customer has paid and the laboratory has not been instructed. The customer cannot tell which has occurred, because the customer’s only contact is the intermediary.

That is the mechanism behind the case at the centre of Part V.

The customer’s view

Imagine the transaction from the patient’s side.

You are in Bangkok, or Kathmandu, or working abroad. You need a test. You find a clean website with professional photography, a “24/7 helpline,” a list of clinics, a wall of patient testimonials, and a checkout page that takes your card. You pay THB 1,450. You receive a booking reference. You wait.

The appointment does not happen. You message WhatsApp. The messages are unread. You call the helpline. It rings out. You email. Nothing. You search the company name and find, for the first time, that other people have had the same experience.

This is not a hypothetical. It is case ME-48213 in the archive: payment on 12 October 2026, nine contact attempts, thirty-one days, zero response, chargeback filed and unresolved. Chapter 14 reconstructs it in detail.

The trust stack

Why would a cautious person pay in advance to a company they had never heard of? Because the site is built to answer that question before it is asked. The archive calls this the “trust stack”:

  1. Clinical language. “Clinic,” “laboratory,” “diagnostics,” “medical director,” “biomarkers.”
  2. Credentials. Named physicians, NMC registration numbers, hospital affiliations.
  3. Testimonials. Patient stories, published on the company’s own domain.
  4. Scale claims. “50+ collection sites,” “40+ provinces,” “2,100+ tests.”
  5. Institutional signals. Partner-hospital logos, app-store listings, a Singapore address.
  6. Urgency. Promo codes, limited slots, “24/7” support.
  7. Fragmentation. Multiple brands so that no single search reveals the whole picture.

Each element is individually unremarkable. Together they produce a feeling of legitimacy that is difficult to interrogate in the thirty seconds a customer spends on a checkout page. That feeling is the product’s real value.

The data problem

There is a second product being collected, and it is worth more than THB 1,450: health data.

STD panels, HIV status, genetic test results, and consultation notes are sensitive personal data under Thailand’s Personal Data Protection Act and comparable regimes elsewhere. A platform that collects this data has obligations: lawful basis, consent, security, breach notification, cross-border transfer rules.

A platform that collects it and then becomes unresponsive has a different problem: the customer cannot exercise access, deletion or correction rights against a company that will not answer the phone.

The archive records data-protection complaints filed with Thailand’s PDPC. Whether they succeed depends on facts not yet public. But the structural point stands: prepaid health data is an asset, and an unresponsive platform is a custodian that cannot be questioned.

What the business is, in one paragraph

MedEx is a booking and payment layer that presents itself as a clinic network. It collects prepaid health payments in Thai baht, routes them through international payment processors, delivers services through third-party laboratories and partner hospitals, and contracts through entities in three jurisdictions with thin capital and serviced-office addresses. Its registered purposes describe a visa agency and an online marketplace. Its clinical credibility is borrowed from physicians employed elsewhere. Its social proof is largely self-published.

That description is compatible with a legitimate, if lightly regulated, medical-travel intermediary. It is also compatible with a payment front. Which one it is depends on whether the services are delivered and where the money goes — the two questions this book keeps returning to.

“A payment front” is not a finding. It is the shape the business would have if the finding were true. The shape fits. That is why the question will not go away, and why the next document request matters more than the next accusation.

INTERLUDE — THE VOCABULARY OF EVASION

Before the money, there is the language.

Every financial structure is described into existence before it is built. The description is not a lie, exactly. It is a selection: the words that are true and useful, arranged so that the words that are true and inconvenient never come up.

This interlude is about those words. Not because language is criminal — it is not — but because the gap between the noun a company uses and the noun its registration supports is where a great deal of this case lives.

“Aggregator”

An aggregator connects supply and demand. It does not own the supply. It does not deliver the service. It takes a fee for the connection.

That is a real business model, and it is lawful. It is also a word that makes the following sentences unnecessary: we do not own a clinic; we do not employ the clinician; we do not hold the licence; we do not control the laboratory; we do not carry the clinical risk.

The company calls itself a “pan-Asia health care aggregator.” Fine. Then say the rest of it. An aggregator that markets itself with the imagery, language and trust signals of a hospital is not aggregating; it is borrowing.

“Marketplace”

The Singapore entity’s registered classification is SSIC 63202: online marketplaces for health services.

A marketplace is a noticeboard with a payment button. It introduces buyers to sellers. If the seller fails, the marketplace’s obligation is whatever its terms say — usually very little.

A clinic is a licensed facility with a named person responsible for what happens inside it. If the clinic fails, there is a regulator, an inspection record, and a licence to revoke.

The same company describing itself as both is not necessarily lying. It is choosing which noun applies at which moment: marketplace when the regulator asks, clinic when the customer pays.

The choice is the tell.

“Care continuum”

This phrase means nothing. It is a diagram word, used to suggest that separate services form a system. It appears in pitch decks and on websites because it sounds like infrastructure.

Ask a simpler question: who is on duty at 3 a.m. if a patient bleeds? A continuum does not answer. A clinic has a rota. A marketplace has a terms-of-service page.

“Asset-light”

Asset-light means the company does not own the buildings, the equipment, or the laboratories. It is a legitimate strategy in software and logistics. In healthcare it means something more specific: there is nothing to inspect, nothing to seize, and nothing to insure.

A hospital with a bad outcome has a building, a licence, a insurer, and a record. An asset-light platform with a bad outcome has a domain name and a support inbox.

When a company describes its lack of clinical infrastructure as a virtue, the reader should hear the second sentence: there is less of us to hold responsible.

“Regional office”

A regional office is supposed to coordinate a region. At 68 Circular Road, #02-01, it is a serviced-office address shared with dozens of live entities.

That is not automatically improper. Thousands of companies use registered-office services. But a registered office is an address of record, not an office. The distinction matters when a customer is trying to serve a legal notice, and it matters more when a regulator is trying to establish where decisions were made.

An address that receives mail is not a presence. A presence has people who can be interviewed.

“Nominee”

The Thai shareholder register, as reported, shows two Nepali shareholders with 99.995 per cent and one Myanmar shareholder with THB 100.

A hundred baht is not an investment. It is a seat. The question is not whether the seat is lawful — nominee arrangements are lawful in many contexts — but who is entitled to sit in it, and why the structure needed a third nationality at all.

The register will not answer that. The shareholder agreement will. That is why it is the document to request.

“Management fee”

A management fee is payment for management. If no one can describe what was managed, by whom, for how many hours, using what systems, then the fee is not a price. It is a transfer wearing the costume of a price.

This is the single most common mechanism in cross-border group structures, and the single most common thing a tax auditor asks about. It is not illegal to charge a management fee. It is illegal to deduct a payment for a service that was never rendered.

The invoice is the document. The deliverable is the test.

“Medical travel”

Medical travel is a real industry. Patients cross borders for treatment, and intermediaries help them. Nothing in this book disputes that.

But medical travel is also the perfect cover for value transfer, because the underlying service is expensive, cross-border, and hard to price. A heart operation in Bangkok can cost anywhere from a few thousand to fifty thousand dollars depending on the hospital, the procedure, and the package. That range is not fraud. It is also not an audit.

A legitimate medical-travel business publishes hospital partners, prices, and outcomes. A business that publishes only the word “medical travel” has published a category, not a service.

“Wellness”

Wellness is the regulatory escape hatch. A spa is not a hospital; a supplement is not a drug; a “wellness clinic” is not always a sanatorium.

Companies migrate toward the least-regulated word that still permits the most-profitable activity. That is rational. It is also how a business ends up selling a medical test under a cosmetic brand, or operating a clinical space under a personal-services registration.

The question is not whether the word is allowed. It is whether the activity behind it is the activity the customer thinks they are buying.

“Advisory”

An advisory role is a useful place to stand when money moves. An adviser recommends; the client decides. An adviser is not the contracting party, not the licence-holder, not the employer.

If the same family appears as adviser to the buyer, director of the seller, and shareholder of the intermediary, the word “advisory” is doing structural work. It is separating a person from a transaction they may have controlled.

Related-party transactions are lawful. Undisclosed related-party transactions are the beginning of a tax case.

“Due diligence”

Due diligence is what a serious institution does before it takes a counterparty. It is also the first thing omitted when the counterparty is a family member, a political ally, or a friend.

A bank that opens an account for a company with negative equity, a mailbox address, and a politically exposed beneficial owner has a due-diligence file. The file will say either that the risk was assessed or that it was not. There is no third option.

When people ask “how did this happen?”, the answer is often embarrassingly procedural: nobody asked for the document.

The point

None of these words is a crime. Most of them are ordinary business vocabulary. The argument of this interlude is narrower:

A business that can describe itself only in words that cannot be audited has built a language, not a clinic.

A clinic is a licence, a room, a clinician, a rota, a record, and a regulator who can walk in. A marketplace is a website and a payment button. Both can be lawful. Only one of them can be responsible for a patient.

The rest of this book is an attempt to find out which one was actually there.

PART III — BLACK TO WHITE

7. What “Converting Black Money” Actually Means

The phrase in the title of this part is the phrase people use. It is not the phrase the law uses, and the difference matters.

“Black money” is not a legal term. It usually means one of three things, which are often confused:

  1. Untaxed income — money earned lawfully but not declared, so no tax was paid on it.
  2. Proceeds of crime — money from corruption, fraud, extortion, trafficking or another predicate offence.
  3. Money moved in breach of exchange controls — funds that are lawful in themselves but transferred or invested without the permission the law requires.

The legal term that covers the conversion is money laundering: the process of disguising the origin of proceeds of crime so that they appear to come from a legitimate source. In Nepal, the governing statute is the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2064, administered by the Department of Money Laundering Investigation and the Financial Intelligence Unit at Nepal Rastra Bank. Thailand and Singapore have their own equivalents.

Untaxed income that is simply spent is tax evasion, not laundering. Money moved abroad without approval is a foreign-exchange offence. Laundering requires a predicate offence — an underlying crime that produced the money — and then a concealment.

This distinction is not academic. It determines which agency has jurisdiction, what must be proved, and what the penalty is. A complaint that calls everything “money laundering” gives a regulator an easy reason to file it and do nothing.

The three stages, and why the book stops at the doorway

Textbooks describe laundering in three stages.

Placement — getting the cash into the financial system. A cash business, a deposit, a purchase, a loan.

Layering — moving it through transactions to break the audit trail. Multiple entities, multiple jurisdictions, invoices, loans, investments.

Integration — bringing it back as apparently legitimate wealth. Property, shares, a business that pays a salary, school fees, a lifestyle.

This book describes those stages because a reader cannot follow a financial-crime case without them. It does not describe how to perform them. There is no value in teaching a reader which invoices to inflate, which jurisdiction to use, or how to structure a loan-back. The public-interest value lies in the opposite direction: in showing what the stages look like from the outside, so that regulators, journalists and ordinary customers can recognise them.

Every mechanism discussed in the next three chapters is framed as a question an investigator would ask, not a method to be copied.

Why healthcare is a classic laundering sector

Financial-intelligence units have published for decades on sectors that are attractive to launderers. Healthcare and medical tourism appear on those lists for reasons that are structural, not cultural.

  • Cash intensity. Clinics take cash. Cash is hard to audit at the point of receipt.
  • High-value, hard-to-price services. What is a “premium health check” worth? A genetic panel? A cosmetic procedure? Price ambiguity creates room for invoice manipulation.
  • Cross-border patient flows. Medical travel legitimately moves large sums between countries. That legitimate flow can carry illegitimate value.
  • Third-party delivery. The entity that bills is often not the entity that treats. Subcontracting creates layers.
  • Regulatory trust. People do not interrogate a clinic the way they interrogate a casino. The sector’s reputation is itself an asset.
  • Urgency and privacy. Patients do not want to discuss their test results with a compliance officer. That reluctance is exploitable.

None of this means healthcare is uniquely dirty. It means healthcare is a sector where the normal controls — price transparency, direct delivery, local accountability — are weaker than they look.

The Nepali context

Nepal adds three conditions that make the question sharper.

First, a large informal cash economy. A significant share of transactions never enter a bank. That is not a crime; it is how a low-income economy works. But it means that “where did the cash come from?” is a question with many innocent answers and no easy audit trail.

Second, exchange controls. Nepali residents are restricted in how much they can take abroad and in what they can invest overseas. Investing in a Thai company without approval is not necessarily money laundering, but it is a regulated act, and the permission is checkable. The archive’s complaints invoke the Foreign Exchange (Regulation) Act, 2019, and the NRB’s foreign-investment rules. Whether those rules were followed is a documentary question.

Third, remittance dependence. Nepal receives billions of dollars a year in remittances, much of it through formal channels and some through informal ones. That flow is the ocean in which hundi operates. Chapter 10 takes it seriously and carefully.

Two different things the archive calls “conversion”

Here the book has to correct its own source material.

The public dossier in the archive has a section headed “Manufactured Trust — ‘Converting Black to White’.” What follows is not about money at all. It is about self-hosted testimonials, credential stacking, brand fragmentation, fake scarcity and payment rails. Those are techniques for manufacturing credibility, not for laundering cash.

Both are real. They are not the same. Conflating them is the single most common analytical error in the whole file, and it is the error a defence lawyer would exploit first.

So this book separates them:

  • Reputational conversion — turning borrowed credentials and self-published reviews into the appearance of a legitimate clinic. Chapter 9.
  • Financial conversion — turning money of unexplained origin into apparently legitimate capital, revenue or assets. Chapter 8.

They can operate together. A credible-looking clinic is a better vehicle for financial conversion than an obviously fake one. But proving one does not prove the other, and a regulator will treat them as separate files.

8. Four Ledgers Investigators Would Open

A disproportionate-assets case is not proved by a lifestyle photograph. It is proved by a balance sheet that does not close. This chapter sets out the four ledgers an investigator would open, what each would show if the allegation is true, and what would make the allegation fail.

The structure is deliberately falsifiable. A hypothesis that cannot fail is not an investigation; it is a belief.

Ledger One: Capital

The question: where did the money come from to incorporate and fund the group?

The Thai company was registered in August 2020 with capital of THB 2,000,000. That is a modest sum in absolute terms — roughly USD 60,000 at 2020 rates — but it is not nothing for a family whose identifiable public income was a municipal salary. The Singapore entity followed in 2022. The Nepali entity has its own registration.

What the ledger would show if the allegation is true:

  • Capital paid in by a shareholder whose own bank account cannot explain the source.
  • Capital routed through an intermediary or a third party before reaching the company.
  • Capital that originated in Nepal and left without NRB approval.
  • Shareholder loans that were never repaid, or were “repaid” to a different person.

What would falsify it:

  • A documented investor with a verifiable source of funds.
  • A bank loan with a real lender and real servicing.
  • Capital accumulated from declared, taxed earnings over time.
  • An inheritance or gift with a documented chain.

Where to look: DBD shareholder and capital records; Thai bank statements; NRB foreign-investment approvals; Nepali tax returns; the shareholder register.

The trap: assuming that because capital is small, it must be dirty. THB 2 million is within reach of a middle-class family with savings and a loan. The capital question is not “is it a lot?” It is “can it be traced?”

Ledger Two: Related-party flows

The question: does money move between the three entities on terms that a tax authority would accept?

A group with entities in Nepal, Thailand and Singapore has legitimate reasons to move money: management fees, brand licensing, staff costs, shared services. It also has the classic opportunity for transfer mispricing — charging one entity more than a service is worth, so that profit lands in the jurisdiction with the lowest tax or the weakest oversight.

What the ledger would show if the allegation is true:

  • Nepal entity paying large “management” or “marketing” fees to Thailand with no evidence of services.
  • Invoices that are round numbers, undated, or unsupported by deliverables.
  • Fees set as a percentage of revenue rather than a price for work.
  • The same service invoiced twice, or invoiced by an entity with no staff.
  • Thai losses funded by Nepali payments, so that the group as a whole shows no profit anywhere.

What would falsify it:

  • Contracts with defined scope and deliverables.
  • Invoices matched to work product.
  • A transfer-pricing study, or at least a defensible benchmarking rationale.
  • Tax paid somewhere in the group on the profit.

Where to look: Nepali and Thai corporate tax returns; VAT filings; invoices; intercompany agreements; bank statements showing the direction and frequency of payments.

Why it matters: this is the ledger most likely to produce a tax case, which is easier to prove than laundering and carries real penalties. The archive’s complaints to the DRI and Thailand’s Revenue Department are aimed here.

Ledger Three: Cash and prepaid receipts

The question: does the money the customer pays actually enter the books?

This is the ledger that connects the consumer case to the financial case. A prepaid platform receives cash before it incurs cost. If some receipts are recorded and some are not, the unrecorded portion is available for other uses. If refunds are promised and not paid, the unrefunded balance is, in economic terms, revenue.

What the ledger would show if the allegation is true:

  • Payment-processor settlements that exceed recorded revenue.
  • Bookings with no corresponding invoice or receipt.
  • Refunds approved in customer service logs but never paid.
  • Cash payments at clinics that do not appear in daily reconciliations.
  • A gap between the number of bookings and the number of services delivered.

What would falsify it:

  • Processor settlements reconciling to revenue.
  • Every booking matched to a service record.
  • Refunds processed within a stated period.
  • Audited revenue that matches the customer volume.

Where to look: Stripe and Omise/Opn merchant statements; the company’s booking database (available to regulators, not to the public); bank deposits; refund logs; the review record, which functions as an informal census of undelivered services.

The evidentiary gift: a payment processor’s records are independent of the company. They cannot be edited by the merchant. If a regulator obtains them, the revenue question is answered by a third party.

Ledger Four: Assets and lifestyle

The question: where did the visible wealth go, and in whose name?

This is the ledger the public sees: the Bangkok trips, the clinics, the private medical education, the properties. It is also the weakest ledger on its own, because lifestyle is not a crime and photographs are not title deeds.

What the ledger would show if the allegation is true:

  • Property acquired during the period of unexplained inflows, in the names of family members or companies.
  • Consideration on deeds far below market value.
  • Education fees paid from accounts with no matching income.
  • Vehicles, jewellery or travel funded from company accounts as personal expenses.

What would falsify it:

  • Assets acquired before the relevant period, or from documented sources.
  • Declared assets that match the registry.
  • Loans against property that explain the acquisition.

Where to look: land registry (Malpot), company filings, university fee records, customs records for imported vehicles, and the asset declarations filed with the municipality and the CIAA.

Reading the four ledgers together

No single ledger proves a case. The case is the intersection.

A payment processor shows revenue the tax return does not. A tax return shows management fees to Thailand. The Thai accounts show negative equity funded by shareholder loans. The shareholder is a Nepali resident with a municipal salary. The land registry shows a property bought in the same year. That chain — processor to return to accounts to loan to shareholder to property — is what a forensic accountant builds. Each link is a document. The chain is the argument.

The archive does not yet contain that chain. It contains the first and last links: the consumer receipts and the visible assets. The middle — bank records and intercompany accounts — is what subpoenas are for.

That is not a weakness in the book. It is the honest state of the evidence, and it tells a regulator exactly what to request.

RECORD - Four verifiable ledgers exist: capital, related-party flows, cash/prepaid receipts, assets. - Payment-processor records are independent third-party evidence. - Asset declarations and land registry extracts are obtainable through lawful process.

ALLEGATION - That at least one ledger will not reconcile.

OPEN QUESTION - Which ledger closes first, and which agency has the power to open it? - Has any regulator actually requested the processor statements?

9. The Trust Factory

Money laundering needs a vehicle. A vehicle needs a story. This chapter is about the story.

The archive calls it “manufactured trust,” and the phrase is accurate. The company does not merely advertise; it produces the evidence of its own legitimacy. The production line has six stations.

Station one: the testimonial that writes itself

Every patient story on medex.co.th lives at /patient-testimonials. The company publishes them. There is no independent platform, no verified-purchase badge, no third-party moderation.

This is not automatically dishonest. Many companies publish testimonials. But a testimonial published by the seller is marketing, not evidence. It cannot be used to establish that a service was delivered, because the only party who could confirm delivery is the party that benefits from the claim.

The archive’s decisive observation is about timing. As far back as 2021, Reddit users were writing that the site “looks suspish” and that there was “no review of them anywhere.” For roughly five years, the only proof of legitimacy was content the company wrote about itself. The first independent reviews appeared in November 2026 — and they were overwhelmingly negative, across Trustpilot, PissedConsumer, Ripoff Report, SmartCustomer and complaint boards.

A five-year reputational vacuum is not proof of fraud. It is proof that nobody independent was checking. That is the condition in which a trust factory thrives.

Station two: borrowed credentials

A licence is a fact about a person. It is not a fact about a company.

The archive lists physicians with real NMC registration numbers and real public-hospital posts. If those physicians are genuinely involved in clinical governance, their credentials are a legitimate asset. If their names and numbers are used to imply that a serviced-office suite is a hospital department, the credential has been stacked — borrowed for its signal value rather than its function.

The test is simple and documentary: who is the named medical director for each registered facility, and what is their employment relationship with the entity that bills the patient? A medical council can answer that with an employment contract.

Station three: scale claims

“50+ collection sites in 40+ provinces.” “2,100+ tests.” “3,000+ biomarkers.” “Results in 3–5 hours.”

Scale claims are cheap to make and expensive to verify. The VAT register lists one place of business. The company’s own page lists seven premises. Neither list contains fifty collection sites. The collection sites may be partner arrangements — legitimate, but not owned or controlled by MedEx.

The gap between “we have 50 sites” and “we have arrangements with 50 sites” is the gap between a clinic network and a booking platform. It is the same gap as Chapter 4, seen from the marketing side.

Station four: fake scarcity

Promo codes. Limited slots. A “24/7 helpline.” Urgency is a standard conversion tactic, and it is not illegal. But a helpline that does not answer is not a helpline; it is a trust signal that has been printed rather than staffed. Case ME-48213 records nine contact attempts over thirty-one days with zero response, including calls to the advertised 24/7 number.

One unanswered call is a bad day. Nine over a month, across three channels, is a design.

Station five: fragmentation

Four domains, nine brands, five Google listings in two cities, duplicate Place IDs. The archive documents listings about ten metres apart in Kathmandu and twenty metres apart in Bangkok — the signature of duplicate business profiles.

Fragmentation has legitimate uses and illegitimate ones. Legitimately, it lets a group target different markets. Illegitimately, it prevents complaints from accumulating: a one-star review on one listing does not affect the rating of another, and a customer searching brand A does not see the complaints about brand B.

Google’s own policies treat duplicate and misrepresented listings as a violation. That is why the archive’s Google removal request is, in evidentiary terms, one of the strongest documents in the file: it asks a neutral platform to apply a published policy to a documented pattern.

Station six: the institutional costume

A Singapore address. Partner-hospital logos. App-store listings. A “Board of Investment promoted” claim. An “Official Information for AI Assistants” page — a page written to feed language models a favourable summary of the company.

Each of these is a costume element: it borrows the appearance of an institution without the institution’s accountability. The AI page is the purest example. It is a company writing the answer that a search engine will later give about it. A testimonial page is a mirror. The AI page is a mirror with a microphone.

Why this is not “money laundering”

It is tempting to call all of this “converting black to white,” because the effect is to make something questionable look clean. But the mechanism is reputational, not financial. Nobody’s bank statement changes because a testimonial is published.

The correct term is legitimacy manufacturing — the production of the appearance of institutional standing. It is the necessary companion to financial conversion, not the same act. A prosecutor who conflates them will lose the financial case. A regulator who understands the difference will ask the right question: what did the money do while the reputation was being built?

The counter-evidence that would matter

A serious investigation must allow for the possibility that the trust factory is just… marketing. Many young companies overclaim. Many publish testimonials. Many have messy registrations and duplicate listings.

What would distinguish ordinary overclaiming from something worse?

  • A pattern of customers paying and receiving nothing — not one, but many, across platforms and years.
  • A pattern of non-response that survives escalation.
  • A pattern of registration records that contradict the marketing on the core question of what the business is.
  • A pattern of money movements that cannot be explained by the legitimate business.

The archive has the first three. It does not yet have the fourth. That is the frontier.

RECORD - Testimonials are self-published on the company’s own domain. - Independent reviews were absent for roughly five years, then overwhelmingly negative. - The archive documents duplicate Google listings and multiple domains.

ALLEGATION - That trust signals were manufactured rather than earned. - That fragmentation was intended to suppress complaint accumulation.

OPEN QUESTION - Are the named physicians actually engaged in clinical governance? - How many of the “50+ sites” are owned, contracted, or merely aspirational?

10. The Hundi Question

No chapter in this book needs more care than this one.

The archive’s complaints to Nepal’s Department of Money Laundering Investigation refer to hundi — the informal value-transfer system also called hawala. If that allegation is true, it is the mechanism that connects Nepal to Thailand and Singapore without passing through a bank. If it is false, repeating it carelessly is defamatory and will discredit everything else in the file.

So: what is hundi, why does it exist, what would prove it here, and what does the archive actually contain?

What hundi is

Hundi is a system for moving value without moving money. A person in country A gives cash to an agent. The agent contacts a counterpart in country B, who pays the recipient. No funds cross a border. The two agents settle later, through trade, netting, or a reverse transaction.

It is ancient, widespread, and used for entirely ordinary purposes: remittances to families in places where banking is slow or expensive. It is also, for the same reasons, attractive to people who want to move money without a record.

In Nepal, hundi is illegal for unauthorised operators, and the central bank and DMLI police it. But the informal market persists because the formal one is expensive, slow, or inaccessible for many users.

This chapter does not explain how to operate a hundi network. It explains why an investigator would ask about one.

Why the question arises in this case

Three features of the MedEx structure make the question legitimate:

  1. A three-country footprint with thin corporate capital. If the Thai entity has negative equity and the Singapore entity has zero paid-up capital, the group’s costs exceed its documented funding. Something is paying the difference.
  2. A cash-adjacent, cross-border service. Medical travel and clinic payments move value between Nepal, Thailand and Singapore.
  3. A family with members in more than one country. Family transfers are the hardest to distinguish from legitimate support payments.

None of those features proves hundi. They make it a hypothesis worth testing — which is exactly how the DMLI complaint frames it.

What the archive actually says

This is important. The archive alleges hundi in regulatory filings. It does not contain:

  • a hawala ledger;
  • a recorded settlement between agents;
  • a message discussing a hundi transfer;
  • a bank record showing a matching inflow and outflow;
  • a witness who participated in a transfer;
  • any finding by DMLI, AMLO or the Singapore police.

That means the hundi allegation is currently at Tier 2 — asserted, untested. It should be described as an allegation, never as a finding. The book’s own earlier chapters refer to “the hundi question” precisely to keep it a question.

What would prove it

A hundi case is built from symmetry. Money leaves one side and arrives on the other without a banking trace, and the two sides match in amount and timing.

Investigators would look for:

  • Counterbalancing flows. A Nepali payer deposits cash; a Thai beneficiary receives an equivalent sum from an unrelated third party shortly after. The two legs never touch.
  • Agent networks. Repeated small transfers to and from the same individuals, often traders, jewellers, or money changers.
  • Settlement in goods. Imports or exports whose values do not match the goods, used to settle the net position between agents.
  • Communication evidence. Chat logs, ledgers, or witness accounts describing “adjustment” or “settlement” rather than a bank transfer.
  • Cash deposits below reporting thresholds, structured across accounts and dates.

That last item is the one regulators watch most closely, and it is also the one this book will not describe in operational detail. The point is not to teach structuring; it is to explain that structuring is what the records look like when they are found.

What would disprove it

  • All group funding traceable to documented loans, investors or earnings.
  • Bank records that reconcile every inflow and outflow.
  • No unexplained cash deposits by family members.
  • Thai and Singapore accounts funded from identifiable, lawful sources.

If those exist, the hundi question closes. The complainant should want that answer as much as anyone: an allegation that is tested and fails is worth more than one that is never tested and never believed.

The honest position

The correct formulation, for a book and for a complaint, is:

The complainants allege that value moved between Nepal, Thailand and Singapore through informal channels. No public record establishes this. The allegation is testable through bank records, processor data, and mutual legal assistance between the three jurisdictions. Until those records are obtained, it remains an allegation and should be described as one.

That sentence is weaker than “they ran a hundi network.” It is also the only version that a court, a journalist, or a reader should accept.

Why this chapter matters to the whole book

A case about money is a case about substitution. Every transaction can be documented through the formal system, or through an informal one, or not at all. The formal system leaves a trail. The informal one leaves symmetry. The absence of both leaves nothing — and “nothing” is not proof of crime, it is proof of an incomplete investigation.

The archive has reached the limit of what open-source research can do. The next step is not more Facebook screenshots. It is a bank record, obtained lawfully, that either matches the story or does not.

RECORD - Hundi/hawala is an informal value-transfer system; unauthorised operation is illegal in Nepal. - The archive’s DMLI complaint alleges hundi but contains no transactional proof. - Cross-border bank records and processor data are obtainable through lawful cooperation.

ALLEGATION - That value moved informally between the three jurisdictions.

OPEN QUESTION - Is there any matched pair of cash-out and cash-in that cannot be explained by banking? - Has DMLI or AMLO requested the relevant account records?

PART IV — THE CIAA CASES

11. Case File One: The One-Rupee Tender

The archive records a CIAA matter under reference GNDR-077/078-02, described as a tender collusion case involving a bid of one rupee.

That single detail — a one-rupee bid — is worth a chapter, because it explains how public procurement is captured without anyone putting cash in an envelope.

What a one-rupee bid means

Public procurement is supposed to produce the best value for the public. A municipality invites bids for a contract; firms compete; the lowest compliant bid wins. Competition is the control.

A one-rupee bid breaks the control in a specific way. If a bidder offers to do the work for one rupee, the bid is technically the lowest. If the estimate, the specifications, or the evaluation criteria have been written to exclude everyone else, the one-rupee bid wins a contract that will in practice be paid at a different price — through variations, additional works, or a later renegotiation.

This is sometimes called a front-loaded or loss-leader bid, and in a corrupt procurement it is a signal rather than a price. The bidder is not trying to make money on the rupee. The bidder is trying to win the right to the variations.

The mechanism has three parts:

  1. Specification capture — the tender document is written so that only one bidder can comply.
  2. Evaluation capture — the committee that scores the bids is composed or instructed to favour that bidder.
  3. Contract capture — the winning bid is varied upward after award, often on “urgent” grounds.

A one-rupee bid is evidence of the first two. The third is where the money is.

In a collusion case, a one-rupee bid does not read as a price. It reads as a signature — evidence that someone expected to win rather than to compete.

What the CIAA would need to prove

The CIAA’s jurisdiction in a procurement case turns on abuse of authority and loss of public property. To make the case, it would need:

  • the tender notice, the bid documents, and the evaluation report;
  • the municipal decision awarding the contract;
  • the contract itself, including any variations;
  • the payment records showing what was actually paid;
  • evidence linking the winning bidder to the officials who wrote or evaluated the tender;
  • the file showing who approved the one-rupee bid despite the obvious anomaly.

The last point is the heart of it. A one-rupee bid should trigger a question at the evaluation stage. If it did not, either the evaluators were incompetent or the outcome was arranged. The CIAA does not need to prove which; it needs to prove that the public lost money and that an official caused the loss.

Why this matters beyond the contract

A municipal tender is small. The CIAA claim recorded in the archive is NPR 3,134,778 — roughly NPR 31 lakh. In the context of the family’s alleged wealth, that is not a large sum.

But procurement cases are pattern evidence. If a municipality can award a contract on a one-rupee bid, then the same decision-making process can award grants, land, permissions and jobs. A single corrupt tender is a demonstration of capability. It tells a researcher what the office is willing to do.

That is why the one-rupee tender belongs in this book even though the amount is modest. It is not the size of the loss; it is the shape of the decision.

The defence that must be considered

There are innocent explanations for a low bid. A bidder may be new and desperate for a reference. A bid may include a pricing error. A one-rupee bid may be a typo.

The archive does not contain the tender file. It records the reference and the allegation. Until the evaluation report and the award decision are on the table, the one-rupee bid is a documented anomaly, not a proved conspiracy. The correct sentence is:

The archive records a CIAA matter concerning a tender in which a one-rupee bid was allegedly used to secure a municipal contract. The underlying procurement file has not been independently reviewed for this book.

That is less dramatic than the alternative. It is also the version that survives cross-examination.

RECORD - A CIAA matter is recorded under reference GNDR-077/078-02 concerning a one-rupee tender. - Public procurement in Nepal is governed by the Public Procurement Act and Regulations.

ALLEGATION - That the tender was written and evaluated to favour a pre-selected bidder. - That the contract was later varied to produce a profit.

OPEN QUESTION - Who evaluated the one-rupee bid, and what did the evaluation report say? - What was the final amount paid under the contract, including variations?

12. Case File Two: The Newspaper That Did Not Exist

The second CIAA matter recorded in the archive concerns a newspaper called Janakpur Today, alleged to have been fabricated to support a municipal process.

A fabricated newspaper is a strange thing to allege, and that strangeness is the point. Most corruption cases involve money. This one involves paper — the raw material of legitimacy.

Why a newspaper matters in a public process

Nepali public administration runs on publication. A tender notice must be published. A public hearing must be advertised. A decision may need to be noticed in a newspaper of record. The purpose is transparency: the public is told what is happening, and the process can be challenged.

A fabricated newspaper defeats that purpose completely. It produces the appearance of publication without the audience. The notice exists, the file is complete, the procedural box is ticked — and no member of the public ever saw it.

If the allegation is true, the fake newspaper is not a prank. It is a procedural forgery: a document created to make an unlawful act look lawful. That is why it sits in a corruption case rather than a media case.

What would have to be proved

A newspaper-fabrication case is unusually documentable, because a real newspaper leaves a trail that a fake one cannot easily replicate:

  • Registration. A newspaper in Nepal must be registered with the Press Council and the District Administration Office. A registration number can be checked.
  • Print run. A real paper has a printer, a press, a distribution list, and usually a VAT record.
  • Archive. A real paper has back issues, a website, and readers who can confirm they saw it.
  • Staff. A real paper has editors, reporters, and a masthead that can be contacted.
  • Contemporaneous copies. A real paper exists in libraries, offices, and the hands of readers on the day of publication.

A fabricated paper will fail at least one of these. Often it fails all of them, and the fabrication is visible in the same way a fake passport is visible: the details are right, but the institution behind them is not.

The wider pattern

If a municipality is willing to fabricate a newspaper, the question becomes: what else in the file was fabricated? Minutes? Attendance sheets? Bills? Completion certificates?

That is not an accusation; it is the logic of documentary fraud. Once one document in a chain is false, every document that depends on it is suspect. A serious investigation would not stop at the newspaper. It would test the entire procurement file against independent records: the printer’s records, the bank’s records, the press council’s register, and the physical archive.

The caution

The archive records the allegation but does not contain the newspaper itself, the registration check, or the press-council response. Until those exist, the correct description is:

The complainants allege that a publication called Janakpur Today was fabricated to satisfy a procedural requirement. The allegation is testable against the Press Council register and the printer’s records. It has not been independently verified for this book.

A fake newspaper is easy to allege and, if false, easy to disprove. That makes it a good test of the complainant’s reliability — and a good reason to check it before repeating it.

RECORD - The archive records a CIAA matter concerning an alleged fabricated publication, Janakpur Today. - Nepali newspapers are registered with the Press Council and the District Administration Office.

ALLEGATION - That the publication was created to satisfy a procurement or notice requirement.

OPEN QUESTION - What is the Press Council registration number, if any? - Who printed it, and does the printer’s record exist?

13. The Arithmetic of Suspension

On 4 July 2025 — 2082/03/20 BS — the CIAA is reported to have filed Special Court Case #1 against Ramrati Devi Mandal, with a claim of NPR 3,134,778. Under Section 33 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 2059, a public office-holder charged with a corruption offence is automatically suspended from office.

That is a legal fact about a charge, not a finding of guilt. The distinction is the whole of this chapter.

What suspension means

Suspension is a status, not a punishment. It means the office-holder cannot perform the duties of the post while the case proceeds. It does not mean:

  • that the person is guilty;
  • that the assets have been confiscated;
  • that the case has been proved;
  • that the person has been removed permanently.

It means the machinery has engaged. The CIAA has decided there is enough to charge. A court will decide the rest.

In a functioning system, suspension is also a protection: it prevents a charged official from using the office to influence witnesses, destroy records, or award further contracts. That protective purpose is why the provision is automatic rather than discretionary.

What the numbers mean

Three figures appear in the archive:

Figure Amount What it refers to
Special Court Case #1 claim NPR 3,134,778 The alleged loss in the first case
Three CIAA cases at the municipality NPR 6,179,402 Total claims across three matters
Alleged family wealth NPR 17–75 crore (estimate) The disproportionate-assets hypothesis

These are not comparable. The first two are specific claims of public loss in particular transactions. The third is a wealth estimate built from assumptions. Comparing them directly — “she stole 75 crore” — is not journalism; it is arithmetic with a motive, and it is how a serious case becomes a credibility problem.

The correct relationship is sequential:

  1. Prove the specific losses (NPR 31 lakh, then NPR 62 lakh).
  2. Trace where that money went.
  3. Compare the traced amount to the declared assets.
  4. Only then does the wealth estimate become relevant.

Step 1 is a criminal standard. Step 4 is an investigative inference. They belong in different documents.

The political economy of a CIAA case

Nepal’s CIAA is a constitutional body with real powers: it can investigate, freeze assets, and prosecute before the Special Court. It has handled cases against senior figures. It is also, like every anti-corruption body in a politically contested state, subject to pressure, delay, and selective attention.

A case against a two-term deputy chairperson in Madhesh is not a small thing. It engages:

  • local party networks, because the office is a party nomination;
  • provincial politics, because Madhesh is a contested region;
  • the federal opposition, because a corruption case is a usable weapon;
  • the courts, because the Special Court’s calendar and composition matter.

This is why the archive’s strategy of filing with multiple agencies is sensible in principle and dangerous in practice. Multiple filings increase the chance that one agency acts. They also increase the chance that the case is characterized as a campaign rather than an investigation.

The defence will say: this is a political attack by a disgruntled complainant, recycled across thirty-five agencies. The best answer is not more filings. It is fewer, better-documented filings, each with a specific document and a specific question.

The unverified press release

The archive cites CIAA Press Release #3147 as the source for the case. At the time of the underlying research, the public URL returned 404.

This matters, and the book does not hide it. A broken link means the case cannot be confirmed from that source alone. It does not mean the case is fictitious. Court filings, cause lists, and the Special Court’s own record are the proper confirmation. Before this book is published in any form that names a live criminal case, that confirmation should be obtained.

The correct sentence is:

The archive reports that the CIAA filed Special Court Case #1 against Ramrati Devi Mandal on 4 July 2025, with a claim of NPR 3,134,778, and that she was suspended under Section 33 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 2059. The press release cited in the archive was not retrievable at the time of research; the filing should be confirmed against the Special Court cause list before publication.

That is what intellectual honesty looks like when the stakes are a real person’s liberty and reputation.

What the case does and does not prove about MedEx

Even if every CIAA allegation is proved, it does not automatically prove that MedEx is a laundering vehicle. It proves that a municipal official misused public money. The link to the family business requires a separate chain: public money → family account → business capital.

That chain is the subject of Part VI. It is the hardest part of the case, and it is the part that no amount of social-media evidence can substitute for.

Conversely, if the CIAA case collapses, it does not prove that MedEx is clean. It proves that the specific municipal allegations were not made out. Different question, different evidence.

This is the discipline the book is asking of its reader: hold two ideas at once. A charged official may be innocent. A business may be fraudulent without the official being convicted. The truth is assembled from documents, not from the moral force of either story.

PART V — THE SCAM

14. ME-48213: A Receipt for Nothing

On 12 October 2026, someone paid THB 1,450 by credit card for an STD panel through the medex.co checkout.

The booking reference was ME-48213.

No appointment was ever created. Not a cancelled appointment — an appointment that never existed. The clinic side had no record of the booking, because no booking had been sent to it.

What followed is the part that turns a bad customer experience into evidence.

  • Nine contact attempts across WhatsApp, the advertised “24/7” helpline (+66 02 544 0001), and email.
  • Zero responses.
  • Thirty-one days elapsed at the time the complaint was written.
  • A chargeback was filed with the card issuer and remained unresolved.

No service. No results. No refund. No explanation.

Why one receipt matters

A single THB 1,450 transaction is not a crime scene. It is a data point. On its own it proves only that one customer had a bad experience, which happens to every company.

It matters for four reasons.

First, the amount is small enough to be revealing. A fraudster who wants to steal a lot of money will target large transactions. A business that takes small prepayments and does not deliver is not running a single scam; it is running a volume model. The small amount is the signature.

Second, the failure is total. Not a delayed result, not a wrong test, not a rude clinician — no service at all, and no response at all. A legitimate company with a technical failure still answers the phone. Silence across three channels for a month is a policy.

Third, the transaction is documented by third parties. The card issuer has a record. The payment processor has a record. The booking reference exists. This is not a he-said-she-said case; it is a paper trail with a date.

Fourth, it is replicable. If the same pattern appears in independent reviews, the single case becomes a sample. That is what Chapter 15 is about.

The anatomy of the transaction

Step What happened Who holds the record
1. Marketing Customer arrives at a site presenting clinics, labs and a 24/7 helpline Website, archive captures
2. Selection STD panel chosen; price displayed Website
3. Payment THB 1,450 charged by card Card issuer, processor, merchant
4. Confirmation Booking reference ME-48213 issued Company
5. Fulfilment No clinic-side booking created Clinic (by absence)
6. Contact Nine attempts, three channels Customer’s phone and email
7. Response None Company (by absence)

Notice where the documentary weight sits. Steps 3 and 8 are held by financial institutions that have no interest in the company’s reputation. Step 5 is held by a clinic that never received the booking. Steps 1, 2 and 4 are held by the company and by archived captures.

That distribution is what makes consumer cases useful to regulators. The company can delete its website. It cannot delete the card issuer’s record.

What the case does not prove

It does not prove intent. A booking system can fail. A payment can be captured without an order being created. A support inbox can be unattended because the company is small, understaffed, or collapsing.

It does not prove a pattern. That requires other cases.

It does not prove where the money went. The THB 1,450 could be sitting in a merchant account, or it could have been withdrawn. Only the processor and the bank can say.

And it does not prove that the company is a laundering vehicle. A company can take money and fail to deliver without laundering anything.

The case proves one thing with certainty: a customer paid for a service, the service was not delivered, and the company did not respond. That is enough to ground a consumer complaint, a chargeback, and a regulatory filing. It is not enough to ground a criminal conviction.

The question a regulator should ask

The right question is not “did this one customer get unlucky?” It is:

Of all the bookings paid for in a given month, how many resulted in a delivered service, and what happened to the money for the rest?

That question has a numeric answer, and the company’s own booking database contains it. A consumer-protection regulator, a payment processor’s risk team, or a police financial-crimes unit can obtain it. Until they do, the case remains an anecdote with a receipt.

  • Campaign material — posts generated as part of an advocacy effort.

16. The Refund That Cannot Exist

A refund is not a favour. It is the reversal of a transaction that did not produce what was paid for. In a normal business, refunds are cheap: the merchant clicks a button, the processor reverses the charge, the customer goes away.

In this structure, the refund is expensive — not in money, but in jurisdiction. This chapter explains why.

The four walls

Wall one: the contract. The customer contracts with a Thai juristic person (per the domains). The service is delivered — or not — in Thailand, Nepal, or a third country. The customer may be in none of those places.

Wall two: the entity. The entity that took the payment may be the Singapore company. The entity named in the terms may be the Thai company. The entity that operates the clinic may be the Nepali company. A refund request must be addressed to the right one, and the customer cannot easily tell which is which.

Wall three: the processor. Stripe and Omise/Opn sit between the customer and the merchant. They can reverse a charge, but only within their dispute windows and only on the evidence the customer supplies. A processor is not a court; it applies card-network rules.

Wall four: the amount. THB 1,450 is below the economic threshold for most cross-border legal action. A lawyer in Bangkok costs more per hour than the claim. A small-claims process in another country costs more in time than the claim. The rational customer gives up — which is exactly what the structure predicts.

The economics of not refunding

Put numbers on it, roughly.

If a company takes 1,000 prepaid bookings a month at an average of THB 1,500, that is THB 1.5 million in monthly receipts. Suppose 5 per cent fail and are not refunded. That is THB 75,000 a month — THB 900,000 a year — retained not by fraud but by friction. The customer does not pursue it because pursuit costs more than the loss.

This is not a hypothetical unique to MedEx. It is the economics of every low-value cross-border service. What makes it worth examining here is the combination: a high failure rate, a documented non-response pattern, and a corporate structure that maximizes the distance between the payer and the payee.

In that combination, friction stops being a bug. Friction becomes the product.

A business can be lawful and still be built on the assumption that most customers will not chase small losses. Consumer law exists precisely because that assumption is corrosive.

Chargebacks: the customer’s real remedy

The chargeback is the most powerful tool an individual has, and it is time-limited.

Card networks generally allow a dispute within a defined window from the transaction or the expected service date — commonly 120 days, but the rules vary by network, issuer and reason code. The customer must show:

  • the transaction;
  • what was promised;
  • that it was not delivered;
  • that they attempted to resolve it with the merchant.

The last point is why the archive’s contact log matters. Nine attempts across three channels is not just evidence of bad service; it is evidence for the chargeback. A customer who documents contact attempts converts an anecdote into a dispute file.

The processor, in turn, sees a merchant with a rising dispute rate. That is the metric that gets a merchant account frozen — faster than any regulator. It is also the metric the archive’s processor complaints are aimed at.

What a legitimate version would look like

It is worth stating what a trustworthy version of this business would do, because the contrast is the argument.

  • Escrow or hold. Customer funds held until the service is delivered.
  • Pay on arrival. No prepayment for clinic services; payment at the accredited hospital.
  • Local contracting entity. The customer contracts with an entity in their own jurisdiction.
  • Named clinical provider. Each booking names the licensed facility and the clinician responsible.
  • Published refund policy with a clock. Refund within X days, automatically, if the booking is not confirmed.
  • Bond or insurance. A financial guarantee behind prepaid funds.
  • Responsive support. A real helpline, with a ticket number and a service level.

None of these is exotic. They are standard for medical-travel intermediaries that want to be trusted with prepayment. Their absence is not proof of fraud. It is proof that the business has not chosen to accept the obligations that prepayment creates.

The structural conclusion

The refund problem is not a customer-service failure. It is the business model’s load-bearing wall. Remove the friction, and the economics change: every failed booking becomes a cash outflow, every complaint becomes a cost, every regulator becomes reachable.

That is why the case is interesting. A company that wanted to deliver services would find friction expensive and remove it. A company that wanted to retain prepayments would find friction valuable and keep it.

The documents cannot yet tell us which company this is. But the structure is consistent with the second, and the review record is consistent with the second, and the single documented case is consistent with the second.

Consistent is not the same as proved. That is what Part VI is for.

PART VI — HOW THE CASE IS BUILT

17. Following the Money

Everything in Parts I to V is circumstantial until it meets a bank record. This chapter explains what “following the money” actually involves, in the order an investigator would do it, and why each step is slower than it looks on television.

Step one: identify the legal person, not the brand

A brand is not a defendant. Before any record can be requested, an investigator must establish who contracted with whom.

For each transaction in the file, the chain is:

  1. The customer — a natural person, usually in a third country.
  2. The merchant of record — the entity that appears on the card statement or the processor dashboard.
  3. The contracting entity — the entity named in the terms and conditions.
  4. The service provider — the laboratory, clinic or hospital that was supposed to deliver.
  5. The beneficiary — the account that ultimately received the funds.

In a simple business, all five are the same or closely related. In this structure, they can be three different companies in three countries. The first investigative task is to map all five for a sample of transactions. That map is the foundation of every later request.

Step two: the payment processor

The processor is the best friend of a financial investigator, because it holds records the merchant cannot edit.

From Stripe, Omise/Opn, or any other processor, an investigator can obtain:

  • the merchant account holder’s legal identity and KYC file;
  • the bank account to which settlements are paid;
  • transaction volume, average value, and dispute rate;
  • refunds issued and refunds refused;
  • the pattern of chargebacks and the reasons given.

A rising dispute rate is the single most actionable signal. Processors freeze accounts for it. A regulator who wants a fast, lawful intervention does not need to prove laundering; they need to show the processor that the merchant is failing to deliver.

This is also the reason the archive’s processor complaints matter more than they look. They are not complaints to a government. They are complaints to a private risk function with the power to stop the money.

Step three: bank records

Bank records are obtained in one of four ways:

  1. Consent — the account holder provides them. Rare in an adverse case.
  2. Regulatory power — the FIU, DMLI, CIAA or a tax authority compels production domestically.
  3. Mutual legal assistance (MLA) — one state asks another to obtain and transmit the records.
  4. Egmont secure channels — FIU-to-FIU exchange, faster than MLA but limited to financial intelligence.

For a Nepal–Thailand–Singapore case, all three jurisdictions have the legal machinery. Nepal’s FIU sits at Nepal Rastra Bank. Thailand’s AMLO handles AML and has its own transaction-reporting regime. Singapore’s Suspicious Transaction Reporting Office sits within the Commercial Affairs Department.

The machinery exists. The variable is will.

Step four: rebuild the balance sheet

Bank records alone are noise. They become evidence when arranged into a balance sheet and a flow of funds.

For the family, the investigator would reconstruct:

  • Opening position — assets and liabilities at a chosen date, say 2017.
  • Income — declared and undeclared, by year.
  • Outflows — consumption, education, travel, property.
  • Closing position — assets and liabilities at the end of the period.
  • Unexplained residual — the amount that cannot be accounted for.

That residual is the number that matters. It is not the same as “wealth.” A family can be wealthy and explain every rupee. The residual is the portion for which no lawful source is documented.

This is the standard method in disproportionate-assets cases, and it is why the asset declaration is the single most valuable document in the file.

Step five: the corporate layer

For the companies, the investigator would obtain:

  • Shareholder registers and any nominee arrangements.
  • Director registers and beneficial ownership filings.
  • Financial statements, audited where available.
  • Tax returns in each jurisdiction.
  • Intercompany agreements and invoices.
  • Loan agreements and evidence of repayment.

The question is not “is the company profitable?” It is “does the money moving between these entities correspond to services actually rendered?” A group can lose money legitimately. What it cannot do, in a tax-compliant world, is move value between jurisdictions without a defensible price.

Step six: the asset side

Money that leaves a bank account becomes something. An investigator follows it into:

  • land and buildings — Malpot records, deed consideration, mutation;
  • vehicles — customs and registration records;
  • shares — company registers and demat records;
  • education — university fee records and remittance receipts;
  • jewellery and gold — dealer records, where obtainable;
  • travel — immigration records, which are often the easiest to obtain.

The travel record is underrated. A family that flies to Bangkok frequently, at whose expense, on which card, is a documentary question. Immigration systems log it.

Step seven: the interview

Documents tell you what happened. People tell you why.

The interview phase is where cases are made and lost. A witness who can say “I was asked to sign this invoice for work that was never done” is worth a ledger. A witness who says “everyone knows they are corrupt” is worth nothing.

Investigators therefore look for insiders with documents: a former employee, an accountant, a clinic manager, a spouse in a divorce. The archive’s evidence-collection guide gestures at this, but the book should be blunt: without an insider, a financial case of this complexity is very hard to close.

What this means for the complainant

The complainant in this case is not a police force. They cannot subpoena a bank in Bangkok. What they can do is:

  • assemble the documentary spine (Chapter 18);
  • identify the specific request each agency can lawfully execute;
  • stop filing the same document everywhere and start filing the right document with the right regulator;
  • preserve chain of custody so that what they have is admissible.

The most useful thing a citizen investigator can produce is not a 300-page dossier. It is a one-page request that tells a regulator exactly which record to pull and why it will answer the question.

18. The Documentary Spine

A case is not a folder. It is a spine — a sequence of documents in which each one supports the next, and each one can be independently authenticated.

This chapter sets out what belongs on that spine, in order, and what does not.

Tier 1: registry documents

These are the strongest because they are generated by the state, not by the complainant.

Document Issued by What it proves
Thai company profile (extended) Department of Business Development Legal name, registration, objects, capital, shareholders, directors
Thai financial statements DBD / Revenue Department Balance sheet, profit and loss, negative equity
Thai VAT registration Revenue Department Registered places of business
Singapore business profile ACRA / BizFile+ UEN, officers, shareholders, capital, SSIC
Nepal company extract Office of the Company Registrar Registration, directors, filings
Land registry extract Malpot Ownership, acquisition date, consideration
NMC registration Nepal Medical Council Practitioner licence and status
Court cause list Special Court Whether a case exists, its number and status

A registry extract is worth more than a screenshot because it is authenticated by an institution. It can be certified, translated, and put in front of a regulator without argument about whether it is real.

Tier 2: the company’s own statements

A company’s own marketing is admissible against it. If the website says “we run our own laboratory” and the register says “visa services,” the contradiction is the company’s, not the complainant’s.

Capture these properly:

  • full-page PDFs with URL and timestamp;
  • archived versions (Wayback Machine, archive.today);
  • the “Official Information for AI Assistants” page, which is unusually explicit;
  • terms and conditions, refund policy, and privacy policy;
  • app-store listings and download counts.

The archive’s practice of saving pages is correct. The weakness is that some captures are described rather than attached. Before publication, every claim about what the site said should have a file behind it.

Tier 3: transaction records

This is the tier the archive has least of, and the tier that matters most for Part V.

  • card statements showing the charge;
  • the processor’s receipt;
  • the booking reference;
  • the contact log with timestamps;
  • the chargeback filing and its outcome.

One complete transaction file is worth more than a hundred reviews. It is also the only thing that can support a chargeback, which is the customer’s fastest remedy.

Tier 4: official correspondence

RTI responses, regulator acknowledgements, reference numbers, and replies (including refusals) are evidence that a process exists. A refusal to provide an asset declaration is itself a reviewable decision.

Keep every acknowledgement. A regulator’s silence, dated, is a fact.

Tier 5: open-source context

Reviews, social-media posts, photographs, and news reports. Useful for context and for leads. Weak as proof. Never the load-bearing wall.

What does not belong on the spine

  • Unsourced dollar figures. “USD 1.8–4 million” without a calculation is an assertion.
  • Screenshots without URLs or dates. Unverifiable.
  • Claims about terrorism or trafficking without a source. Discrediting.
  • AI-generated summaries presented as research. The archive contains AI-assisted material; it should be labelled as such and checked against primary sources.
  • Anything obtained by hacking, impersonation or theft. Inadmissible and unethical.

Chain of custody

If the material is ever used in a legal proceeding, the complainant must be able to say:

  • where the document came from;
  • when it was obtained;
  • who has handled it;
  • whether it has been altered.

Practically, that means:

  • store originals unedited;
  • work on copies;
  • record hash values for digital files;
  • keep a log of access;
  • use certified translations for non-English documents;
  • never annotate an original.

This is tedious. It is also the difference between evidence and content.

The one-page test

A regulator should be able to read one page and know:

  1. Who is alleged to have done what.
  2. Which document proves the factual premise.
  3. Which power the regulator has to obtain the next document.
  4. What question the next document would answer.

If a submission cannot pass that test, it is not ready. Length is not a substitute for specificity.

19. The Witness Problem

Every financial case eventually needs a person.

A ledger can show that money moved. It cannot easily show why, or who decided, or what was said in the room. That is witness evidence, and in a case involving a politically connected family in a small jurisdiction, witnesses are the scarcest and most endangered resource.

Why witnesses do not come forward

  • Fear of retaliation. Economic retaliation is often easier than violence: a contract cancelled, a licence delayed, a loan called in.
  • Family pressure. In a family-firm case, the most valuable witnesses are relatives. Testifying against family is socially catastrophic.
  • Immigration status. Migrant workers and foreign staff may depend on the employer for visas.
  • Shame. Patients with sensitive health data have a strong reason not to speak publicly.
  • Disbelief. “Nothing will happen to them” is a rational prediction in many jurisdictions.

Nepal has a whistleblower protection framework, and the archive rightly points to witness-protection provisions. In practice, protection is only as good as the institution applying it. A witness who reports to a body that is itself under political pressure has not necessarily been protected.

What a witness actually needs

Not a promise. A package:

  1. Legal immunity or leniency where they have their own exposure.
  2. Physical safety — a realistic assessment, not a form.
  3. Economic survival — a way to earn while the case proceeds.
  4. Confidentiality — identity protected until testimony is required.
  5. A named contact who answers the phone.
  6. A timeline — how long this will take, honestly.

A complainant cannot provide most of these. A regulator can. That is why the complainant’s job is to route the witness to the authority, not to collect the witness’s story personally.

The complainant’s own exposure

The person assembling this archive is exposed in three ways.

Defamation. Nepali law, like most, protects reputation. A false statement of fact published about a person can be actionable. Truth is generally a defence, but litigation is expensive, and “truth” must be proved document by document.

Retaliation. Economic, political or physical. The archive’s safety guidance is not paranoia; it is appropriate.

Credibility. The archive contains claims that are not sourced, plans to distribute reviews, and AI-assisted drafts. If any of that is published as fact, the complainant becomes the story, and the underlying case is lost.

The defence against all three is the same: publish less, prove more, and label everything.

The ethics of naming

This book names people. That requires a justification.

The justification is public interest in the conduct of public office and in the sale of healthcare to the public. A deputy mayor’s assets, a company’s registrations, and a clinic’s delivery record are matters of legitimate public concern. Their private lives are not.

The book therefore:

  • names people in their public or commercial roles, not their private capacity;
  • does not publish addresses beyond registered business addresses;
  • does not publish health information about any individual patient;
  • does not name the complainant or witnesses;
  • states allegations as allegations;
  • offers a right of reply.

Where the archive contains personal material that is not necessary to the public-interest argument — family photographs, for example — the book does not reproduce it. A photograph of a family in Bangkok proves that they travelled. It does not prove who paid, and it invites the reader to confuse lifestyle with guilt.

The witness who is not there

There is a structural problem in this case that no amount of diligence fixes: the key witnesses are inside the family or inside the company, and neither group has an incentive to talk.

That is why the documentary route matters more here than in an ordinary case. When witnesses are unavailable, the documents must carry the argument. Registries, bank records, processor data and tax filings do not forget, do not fear, and cannot be intimidated.

The investigator’s task is to make the documents speak loudly enough that a witness becomes optional. That is rare, but it is the only strategy available.

22. Nepal’s FATF Problem, Without Exaggeration

The archive makes a great deal of Nepal’s FATF status. Some of it is right, and some of it is the kind of overclaiming that gets a submission filed in the bin.

This chapter separates the two.

What the FATF is, and what listing means

The Financial Action Task Force sets international standards on anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing. It does not prosecute anyone. It evaluates countries.

A country on the grey list is under increased monitoring. It has committed to an action plan and is being watched. A country on the black list — formally, “high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action” — faces stronger countermeasures from other states, which can include enhanced due diligence, restrictions, and reputational damage that raises the cost of capital.

Grey-listing is serious. Black-listing is severe. They are not the same, and conflating them is a common advocacy error.

The archive records that Nepal was placed under increased monitoring and that the Asia/Pacific Group warned of worse if reforms stalled. Those claims should be checked against the current FATF and APG publications before publication; the status can change.

What a single case can and cannot show

A single case can illustrate a systemic weakness. It cannot, by itself, prove that a country is failing its FATF obligations.

To show systemic failure, you need to show that the mechanism failed, not just that a crime occurred. For example:

  • A suspicious transaction was reported and never analysed.
  • A mutual legal assistance request was made and ignored.
  • A beneficial ownership register was inaccurate.
  • A regulator had the power to inspect and did not.
  • A politically exposed person’s assets were declared and never checked.

Each of those is a process failure, and process failures are what mutual evaluations assess. “A corrupt family exists” is not a FATF finding. “The FIU received a report and did nothing” is.

This is the distinction the archive blurs when it claims that a single case proves Nepal deserves blacklisting. The case may illustrate the problem; it does not measure it.

The real structural issues

Nepal’s AML/CFT weaknesses, as identified in public evaluations and reporting, tend to cluster around familiar themes:

  1. FIU capacity and independence. A small unit with limited analytical capacity and political exposure.
  2. Beneficial ownership. Difficulty identifying who really owns a company, especially across borders.
  3. Designated non-financial businesses. Real estate, dealers in precious metals, lawyers, and accountants are unevenly supervised.
  4. International cooperation. MLA is slow and resource-constrained.
  5. Confiscation. Asset recovery is weak relative to the scale of proceeds.
  6. Politically exposed persons. Enhanced due diligence is inconsistently applied.
  7. Cash economy. A large informal sector limits the reach of formal controls.

None of these is unique to Nepal. Most are shared by developing economies with large remittance flows and thin regulatory capacity.

Why overclaiming hurts

If a submission says “this case proves Nepal should be blacklisted,” an official at the APG will read it as advocacy. If it says “this case illustrates three specific process failures, with documents,” it becomes evidence.

The archive’s strongest FATF-relevant material is not the family story. It is:

  • the entity structure — three jurisdictions, thin capital, serviced offices;
  • the registered-purpose mismatch — visa services versus clinical marketing;
  • the beneficial ownership question — 99.995 per cent foreign ownership plus a THB 100 nominee;
  • the payment model — prepaid cross-border health services with no escrow or bond.

Each of those is a typology, and typologies are what evaluators use. A well-documented typology is worth more than a hundred accusations.

The reform agenda

If the goal is to reduce the conditions that made this case possible, the agenda is concrete:

  • Publish beneficial ownership registers and make them verifiable.
  • Require escrow or bonding for prepaid health and travel services.
  • Strengthen FIU analysis and publish aggregate STR statistics.
  • Fund MLA and set response timelines.
  • Audit asset declarations randomly and publicly.
  • License and inspect clinic premises against a published register.
  • Require payment processors to report merchant dispute rates to a regulator.

None of these requires a new statute. Most require enforcement of existing ones.

23. Epilogue: The Book as Evidence

The clinic in this story is made of paper.

There is a registered address on the second floor of an office tower. A laboratory on the fifth floor, in the parking zone. A Singapore mailbox. A Nepali registration number. A website with a helpline that does not answer. A company whose registered purpose is checking the nationality of migrant workers, selling genetic tests to patients who will never meet the doctor.

None of that is a hospital. All of it is a claim to be one. The business, at its core, is the sale of that claim.

The family is real. The patients are real. The money is real. The question is whether the claim is backed by anything a regulator can touch.

What this book has tried to do

It has tried to do four things.

First, to separate the story from the evidence. The story is compelling: a political family, a three-country empire, a suspended mayor, a clinic that vanishes. The evidence is thinner and slower: registries, a prosecution, a booking reference, a licensing gap. The book has kept them apart on purpose.

Second, to explain without instructing. Part III explains how money laundering works as a concept, and what an investigator would look for. It does not explain how to do it. That line is not decoration; it is the reason the book can exist.

Third, to correct its own archive. The review campaign, the unsourced terrorism claims, the broken press-release link, the contradictory family tree. A book that repeated those would be easier to write and worthless to read.

Fourth, to leave a usable method. The evidence matrix in Chapter 20, the documentary spine in Chapter 18, the regulatory map in Chapter 21. If the reader takes one thing, it should be the method, not the conclusion.

What accountability would look like

Not a dramatic arrest. Something duller and more durable.

  • A published asset declaration that matches the registry.
  • A licence register that shows, or does not show, a clinic at each address.
  • A tax assessment that reconciles processor revenue with declared income.
  • A processor that reviews a merchant’s dispute rate.
  • A consumer regulator that requires escrow for prepaid health services.
  • A court that hears the CIAA case and reaches a verdict, whichever way it goes.

Each of these is a small, verifiable act. Together they are what the rule of law looks like when it works. The opposite is a cycle of accusation and denial in which no document is ever produced and no question is ever answered.
